From Vision to Value: Finding EMET’s First Paying Customer

By Guy Blat · 8 November 2026 · 5 min read

A reflection on building a sustainable business around human-AI collaboration

The constraint I set for myself early

Before we had any clients, before we even had a name for what EMET would become, I wrote something to the team that I still hold myself to: whatever we build has to become a real business, not only an interesting exploration.

Each engagement has to create enough tangible value that an organization is willing to pay for it — while we also learn and develop our own methods, capabilities, and eventually perhaps products, from the experience of actually doing it.

I framed the question we should keep in the room as simply as I could: how can we make a living by helping the future of human-AI collaboration emerge?

Not just how we can describe a compelling vision, but how we can create value that makes the vision economically sustainable.

Why I don’t trust a theory of the market

I could sit down and write a market analysis: who needs this, what they’d pay, how we’d price it. I’ve seen enough of those to know their limitations before anyone has tested them against a real organization with real money and a real deadline.

So when I think about EMET’s first offer, I try to be disciplined about separating two different things: what I believe, and what I actually know.

What I believe is that teams and communities who want to create something together, but struggle to realize the potential of their own collaboration, are promising first customers.

The problem is a gap I see everywhere: knowledge gets lost, ideas don’t develop further, commitments get forgotten, and there isn’t always a space where everyone can contribute at their best.

I believe an empathic AI agent that knows the people and their shared context — one that helps build trust and shared memory without replacing human judgment — is positioned to help address that gap.

But the value we need to demonstrate is not simply that people feel more connected. It is that their collaboration produces better decisions, fewer lost opportunities, stronger follow-through, and more meaningful progress toward goals they care about.

What I don’t yet know is whether we can deliver those outcomes consistently, whether we can do so better than existing alternatives, what an organization would actually be willing to pay, or how to price the service sustainably.

Those aren’t questions I can answer from a chair. They’re questions that only get answered by finding an organization, offering something concrete, and learning from what actually happens.

The shape of a first offer

Within roughly 90 days, I believe we can be ready to offer a paid pilot.

EMET would help a team discover and articulate its shared North Star, understand its members and their perspectives better, and improve how they collaborate toward a common purpose.

Not a finished software product, but a service supported by an evolving AI agent — aimed at something the team is already trying to accomplish and finding difficult.

We have a real first opportunity in view to explore this approach. We’re still aligning internally on what we would offer, what we want to learn, and what a small paid pilot should include.

But I think the most important part is not the offer itself. It is the discipline of the experiment.

We need to understand the organization’s problem in its own words, establish what meaningful improvement would look like, and discover whether our approach can produce it.

We should be able to deliver something valuable even if the relationship ends after the pilot.

And we should be prepared for the possibility that our initial assumptions are wrong.

The goal is not to convince someone to buy our vision. It is to discover whether a specific problem, experienced by a specific organization, is one we can solve well enough to justify a real investment.

What would count as success?

A paid pilot would be an important first signal. It would show that an organization is willing to invest in exploring the approach.

But willingness to try is not the same as demonstrated value.

A stronger signal would be that the organization experiences a meaningful improvement and wants to continue, expand the engagement, or recommend the approach to others.

We should therefore look beyond whether the pilot was completed or whether participants enjoyed the experience.

Did the team develop greater clarity about its purpose? Did previously unheard perspectives become part of the conversation? Were important decisions better understood? Did commitments turn into action? Did the organization make progress that would otherwise have been difficult?

Not every outcome will be easy to measure, and not every valuable change will appear immediately. But we should be able to identify credible evidence of improvement, rather than relying on our own enthusiasm.

Just as importantly, we need to learn whether we can deliver that value repeatedly, without requiring extraordinary effort from the founders each time.

That is where an interesting experiment begins to become a sustainable business.

Letting the business model follow the experience

I’d rather the product and business model grow out of shared experience with real organizations than have us impose a rigid structure in advance.

That’s not indecision. It’s the same instinct that shaped how I think about building organizations generally: create the conditions, and let the right shape emerge from what actually happens inside them, rather than designing it from the outside and hoping reality matches the design.

But allowing something to emerge doesn’t mean abandoning discipline.

It means being explicit about our assumptions, choosing experiments that can challenge them, and learning from evidence rather than defending our original ideas.

It also means recognizing an important distinction: the market can help us discover where our vision creates value, but it should not define the vision itself.

Our commitment to empathy, human agency, trust, and meaningful collaboration is not simply a marketing hypothesis. It is the foundation of what we want to bring into the world.

What remains open is how that commitment becomes useful enough, concrete enough, and repeatable enough to sustain a business.

We don’t need to know the final answer today.

We need to find the next real conversation, the next real problem, and the next opportunity to create value together.

So the discipline I’m trying to hold onto is simple: name the belief, name what’s still unknown, and go find out — with a real organization, a real problem, and a real willingness to pay.

Let the evidence shape the business, without losing sight of why we wanted to build it in the first place.
