The Missing Place: Why Distributed Organisations Need a Game World

By Victor Vorski · 11 November 2026 · 6 min read

Making collaboration visible, navigable, and understandable to humans and AI agents.

The office was more than a workplace. It was a mental model.

After nearly thirty years of exploring, building, and experimenting with remote collaboration and distributed teams, I am beginning to appreciate something that may be one of the most fundamental missing pieces.

We have spent decades building tools for remote collaboration. But we have not adequately solved how people form a mental picture of the organisation they are collaborating in.

This may seem like a relatively small design problem. I increasingly suspect it is foundational.

And with the emergence of AI agents, fractional work, fluid organisations, and the possibility of agentically coordinated ecosystems, it becomes even more important.

Perhaps one of the central things we need to build is not another organisational dashboard or collaboration platform.

Perhaps we need to create a shared, navigable world in which an organisation can become understandable.

A world that humans can intuitively grasp, and AI agents can understand and interact with.

Think about a traditional organisation.

A factory, a restaurant, a school, a hospital, or an office.

The physical space does much more than contain the activity. It helps define the organisation itself.

You walk into a restaurant and rapidly understand a remarkable amount about what is happening. There is a kitchen, a dining area, staff, customers, tables, a menu, and recognisable patterns of responsibility. You may not know the details, but you immediately have a usable model of the whole.

This is not a vague intuition; it is a studied phenomenon. The urban theorist Kevin Lynch spent five years asking residents of Boston, Jersey City, and Los Angeles to sketch their own cities from memory, and found that the cities people navigated well shared the same five ingredients: clear paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks. Lynch called the quality that lets a stranger build an accurate mental map from a short walk legibility. A legible city lets you orient yourself without a guide. An illegible one leaves you dependent on turn-by-turn instructions forever. Organisations have the same property, and most distributed ones score badly on it.

The physical environment communicates. It reveals relationships, activities, territories, expectations, and boundaries. Much of the organisation becomes comprehensible without anyone explaining it.

Humans are embodied creatures. Our cognition developed through interacting with physical environments, recognising patterns, navigating territory, understanding proximity, and reading social situations.

We are exceptionally practised at making sense of places.

This is also why digital games so often create worlds. Usability researchers who study games have shown that the same signals which make a city legible — clear feedback, consistent conventions, visible status — are exactly what separates a game you can pick up in minutes from one that needs a manual. A well-placed landmark, a consistent colour for danger, a minimap that always shows where you are relative to your goal: these let a player enter an unfamiliar system and build a surprisingly sophisticated understanding of how it works, often without reading a single instruction.

We have not brought enough of this thinking into organisational design.

When organisations moved online, we largely replaced physical spaces with documents, chat channels, meetings, task boards, and databases.

These tools helped us communicate and execute work.

But they did not necessarily give us a coherent sense of where we are, what we are part of, or how the whole fits together.

The office disappeared, and with it disappeared an important cognitive interface.

Distributed collaboration has a spatial imagination problem

Consider joining a new distributed organisation.

You receive a welcome document, several links, invitations to different communication channels, perhaps an organisational chart, and access to a project management system.

Somewhere inside these systems is an organisation.

There are people, shared intentions, relationships, agreements, responsibilities, decisions, commitments, projects, and opportunities.

But the organisation itself is remarkably difficult to see. Researchers who map how distributed teams actually work together have found the same thing we see informally all the time: the org chart tells you almost nothing. It shows who reports to whom, not who collaborates with whom or where work actually flows. The map on paper and the territory people inhabit are different places.

You have to reconstruct the real organisation in your mind from fragments.

Who belongs to what? Who is actually active? What are the different teams trying to accomplish? What decisions can they make? What work is waiting for someone? Where do people gather? What matters to them? How do they relate to the wider ecosystem?

Most importantly: where do I fit?

A Space is the missing place, named

This is, more or less, where our own work on what we call the Audax Meta-Framework started — not as an abstract model, but as an attempt to answer the question above directly.

At the centre of it sits a simple idea: a Space is a place of participation with a purpose and a boundary. It could be a Slack channel, a shared document, a kanban board, a repository, or a physical room — the framework does not care which tool a team happens to use, only that the concept of a bounded place for a kind of activity exists and is shared. Alongside it sit Commitments (an accepted responsibility, attributable to someone), Processes (connected activity moving toward a shared purpose, often spanning several Spaces), Cycles (the recurring rhythms — the daily check-in, the sprint, the review), and Flows or Journeys — particular relationships moving through stages over time, like a client engagement or an onboarding.

What matters is the binding: the explicit mapping between a concept like Space and whatever tool actually represents it for a given team. One team’s “Space” is a Discord server; another’s is a conference room with a whiteboard. Once that mapping exists, humans and software can share meaning about collaboration even though they are looking at different surfaces. This is the legibility problem from the restaurant and the city, translated into the vocabulary a distributed, partly-digital, partly-agentic organisation actually needs. A Space with a clear purpose and boundary is a landmark. A Process crossing several Spaces is a path. A Cycle is the rhythm telling a newcomer when to expect the next landmark.

The world needs someone who already lives in it

A map, however well drawn, is a static document — and most organisations don’t need another diagram pinned to a wall. A legible world needs something that moves through it and keeps the picture current.

This is the other half of our thinking, which we call Agent in the Loop. Rather than placing an AI agent outside human collaboration, approving or rejecting actions after the fact, we put it inside the loop of the team’s own work — tracking the Commitments people make to each other, remembering which conversation a decision happened in, and following up gently with whoever owns something that has stalled. Because it already understands Spaces, Commitments, Processes, and Cycles as the same underlying concepts regardless of which tool carries them, it can answer the question a new joiner is really asking — where do I fit, and what’s actually happening here — by walking the world rather than scraping a transcript. And because it sees the whole pattern over time, it can hand the team a kind of telemetry on their own collaboration: where commitments tend to slip, which Spaces go quiet, whether a new way of working is actually helping. This is visibility the team holds for itself, never surveillance from above.

That, I think, is the practical shape of the “game world” I’m reaching for: not a 3D rendering of an office, but a shared, bounded, navigable structure — Spaces, Commitments, Processes, Cycles — legible to a human walking in cold, and equally legible to an agent that lives there and can help everyone find their footing.
