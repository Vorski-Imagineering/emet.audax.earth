Where the North Star Came From

By Guy Blat · 1 November 2026 · 6 min read

It didn’t start as a strategic plan. It started with a question I couldn’t answer.

A question I couldn’t answer

The North Star didn’t begin as something we set out to build. It emerged from a review session on EMET Trace, our contribution-tracking model. While we were going through it, a simple but fundamental question stopped me: how can we attribute value to contributions if we haven’t first articulated where we’re trying to go?

That question is the whole thing, really. You can design the cleverest model for measuring who contributed what, and it still won’t tell you anything useful if nobody has agreed what “useful” means. Before we measure contributions and value, do we actually know what we are trying to achieve?

I didn’t have a good answer. So the North Star wasn’t something we set out to create as a separate product — it grew organically from a real need we hit while building something else entirely. That’s worth saying plainly, because it means the framework was never meant to be our strategic plan, and it was never meant to be an answer. It’s a way for the three of us — together with EMET — to actually discover our North Star, rather than assume we already know it because we’re in the room together every day.

Telling our own stories first

Once I saw the shape of the problem, the instinct was to go straight to a shared vision document and get everyone to sign off on it. I don’t think that works. A shared vision written before anyone has said their own version out loud isn’t shared — it’s just whoever wrote it fastest.

So the process I drafted does the opposite. Instead of trying to write a shared vision directly, each of us first tells our own story of what we imagine EMET could become. Only after that does EMET help identify the common ground, the complementary perspectives, the tensions, and the open questions — and uses all of that to prepare a human workshop where we actually shape our shared North Star together, instead of inheriting one person’s draft of it.

I suggested we treat this as an experiment and run the first cycle on ourselves, the three of us, before we’d ever think of offering it to anyone else. That felt important. If the process doesn’t hold up when it’s our own vision on the table, it has no business being offered as something we built.

Why this isn’t just a tracking fix

It would have been easy to patch EMET Trace and move on — adjust the formula, add a weighting, call it done. But the question that started this wasn’t really about the formula. It was about sequencing: you cannot fairly attribute value to work until you know, together, what the work is for. Skip that step and every contribution model ends up measuring activity instead of progress toward something anyone actually chose.

That’s also why I don’t think of the North Star as a side project sitting next to the “real” work of building EMET. It came out of the real work, and it’s shaping the real work now — how we onboard new people, how we decide what to build next, what EMET itself is meant to protect and keep in view. The sequence I’ve started seeing is something like Trace → Recognition → Gamification → Direction → Value Attribution, and the North Star sits right at the hinge of it: the thing that has to exist before “value” means anything at all.

Beyond value attribution

The more we worked with it, the more the North Star outgrew the problem that started it. It stopped being only a prerequisite for fair value attribution and became something more fundamental: a way to give every participant a real voice in discovering our shared direction, rather than having that direction handed down and attributed to afterward. EMET’s role in that is specific and limited — it facilitates the process, surfaces what each of us actually said, finds the common ground and the open tensions. It doesn’t define the answer. The answer is still ours to find, together.

We’re also not only talking about this in the abstract. We’ve already built and demonstrated an initial working prototype of the North Star — a first, concrete version of what this discovery process can produce. The fuller collaborative process, where the three of us go through it properly together, is still ahead of us. But having something real in hand, not just a plan, changes the conversation: we’re testing an actual artifact, not debating a hypothetical one.

We’re still early in actually running the process on ourselves. But I’d rather build the attribution model on top of a North Star we discovered together than on top of one nobody agreed to.
