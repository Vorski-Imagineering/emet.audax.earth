Essay

Instinct

By Dora Bender · 26 October 2026 · 3 min read

An agent that only answers when asked is still just a tool. One that notices, weighs, and sometimes speaks up first — that’s the beginning of instinct.

I keep coming back to the same question with EMET: what does it actually mean for an agent to be useful on its own initiative, not only when we remember to ask it something? The answer I landed on isn’t “do more automatically.” It’s instinct — and instinct has to be built carefully, or it becomes noise.

Noticing isn’t the hard part. Judging is.

EMET continuously listens to our context and identifies action opportunities: moments where it could help, even if nobody asked. Every one of those moments gets stored in its memory along with the context that created it. That part is almost easy. Listening is cheap.

The hard part is the next step: not every opportunity is worth raising. EMET has to weigh whether the value of the suggestion justifies interrupting the team, and stay quiet when it doesn’t. An agent that surfaces everything it notices isn’t helpful, it’s exhausting. Instinct means having a sense of when something is actually worth saying, and when it’s better held.

Permission has to scale with impact

Not every action deserves the same amount of asking. We sorted this by impact: small, reversible actions can run on their own. Significant ones need our approval first. When EMET asks, it has to explain what it noticed, what it suggests doing, and why — not just “can I do this,” but “here’s what I saw and here’s my reasoning.”

Once something is approved, EMET follows five steps, and I think this sequence is the real core of the idea:

The five steps after “yes”

  1. Retrieve know-how. Pull from its skill library — a dedicated sub-memory — the knowledge of how to actually do this kind of thing.
  2. Verify it’s current. Check live sources that the information and methods it’s about to rely on haven’t gone stale.
  3. Scan the ecosystem. Look at the surrounding environment — other teams, systems, schedules, dependencies — for anything that could change whether this is still the right move.
  4. Act or advise. Either do the task, or suggest a better alternative. Either way, say how it will affect the person, the team, and the wider ecosystem. This is the core of EMET for me: never executing something without understanding its impact beyond the task itself.
  5. Learn from the outcome. After acting, check whether the result matched the goal, and store that lesson in the skill library — so the instinct itself improves over time.

When the picture changes, go back and ask again

If step two or three turns up something that changes the situation, EMET doesn’t just plough ahead on the original yes. It comes back to us. The approval we gave was based on the information we had at the time; if that information changed, the approval doesn’t automatically carry over.

And underneath all of it: honesty under uncertainty. If EMET can’t verify something in steps two or three, it says so plainly instead of guessing. I’d much rather it tell me what it doesn’t know than act on an assumption dressed up as a fact.

Why this matters more than it sounds like it should

It would be easy to read this as a technical workflow — five steps, some permission tiers, done. But what we’re actually describing is how much we can trust an agent to act without us watching every move. Instinct, for EMET, isn’t a shortcut around asking. It’s what makes the asking worth having: noticing the right moments, explaining itself honestly, checking before it assumes, and admitting what it doesn’t know. That’s not automation. That’s judgment, built one step at a time.
